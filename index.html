Count complete flashes

Dark → light → dark is one flash: two opposing transitions. Five cycles per second means five flashes, not five individual color changes.

Evaluate every one-second window

At most three flashes in any one-second period meets the frequency condition. Above that, evaluate the general/red flash definitions and area threshold. The examples below exceed those thresholds.

An e-commerce homepage opens with a promotional banner occupying the viewport and switching between black and white.

Why it fails: five complete cycles fit in one second. Black-to-white changes clearly meet the general-flash luminance definition, and the full-screen flashing area exceeds the area exception.

Full width alone does not prove failure: height and the area actually flashing matter. Here, the full-screen assumption makes the area condition explicit.

Safer replacement: keep the sale banner static. If flashing is retained, ensure no one-second window contains more than three flashes, or verify that it is below the applicable thresholds.

400 × 280reference panel size, pixels

A severe-weather portal uses a large animated warning panel. Its background switches between dark gray and pure red.

Why it fails: four qualifying red flash pairs occur in a second. Assume the entire 400 × 280 panel flashes at WCAG’s 1024 × 768 reference rendering: it can fill a 341 × 256 assessment rectangle and exceeds its 25% area limit.

A tiny flashing red icon is not automatically a failure of 2.3.1. This example specifies a large panel. Red has a separate transition test, rather than a lower numerical flash-count limit. This color pair also qualifies as a general flash.

Safer replacement: use a steady warning panel with a clear heading and severity text. Color can reinforce the message without flashing.

Technical note: saturated-red transitions

WCAG 2.2 describes a working test using R/(R + G + B) ≥ 0.8 for one state and a CIE 1976 UCS chromaticity difference > 0.2 between states. For sRGB #FF0000 and neutral #333333, the ratio is 1 for red and the chromaticity difference is approximately 0.259.

A media portal automatically plays a large hero video with high-contrast lighting effects. In this hypothetical sequence, each flash changes most of the video from a near-black scene to near-white and back.

Why it fails: the 0–1 second window contains four general flashes over an area exceeding the exception. Averaging six flashes across two seconds conceals the violating burst.

Missing pause controls do not establish a 2.3.1 failure, and adding pause controls does not fix excessive flashing. The flash thresholds determine this failure.

Safer replacement: remove or edit the burst and verify every one-second window. A static hero poster avoids flashing; player controls address additional usability needs.

The area exception is local to the visual field

The normative area condition is no more than 0.006 steradians of concurrent flashes within any 10-degree visual field. WCAG’s reference approximation uses a 341 × 256 pixel rectangle at a 1024 × 768 rendering.

One quarter of that rectangle is 21,824 square pixels. Assess the flashing area inside any placement of the rectangle—not a quarter of the entire screen. A large bounding box does not prove that all of its pixels flash.

These are constructed failure scenarios, not analyzed media files. For actual video, measure qualifying transitions, timing, and concurrently flashing area at the relevant display size. The reference calculation is an approximation, not a universal CSS size allowance.
